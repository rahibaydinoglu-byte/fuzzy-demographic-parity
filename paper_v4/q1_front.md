# Abstract

Group-fairness criteria treat protected groups as crisp and do not show where inside a model a disparity arises. Fuzzy demographic parity (FDP) defines the protected group by a membership function such as *young*; it reduces to demographic parity for crisp groups and can be trained as a covariance penalty. For Takagi–Sugeno–Kang (TSK) classifiers, the parity gap splits into a routing term, which arises when groups fire different rules, and a within-rule term. The split shows that penalising within-rule disparity, a learned form of conditional statistical parity, can leave the group gap intact because the model re-routes the groups. We give a sufficient condition for this disparity displacement, a rule base without within-rule disparity that is nearly Bayes-optimal, which extends to regularised, approximately trained models. With known Bayes scores, displacement was complete with trainable antecedents and four or more rules, and absent with one rule. On Adult, the rule-local penalty left the gap at 0.187 against 0.189 unconstrained for sex, and at 0.189 against 0.191 for the fuzzy group *young*, and a mixture of experts without fuzzy antecedents behaved alike. Used as the measurement device, the fairness-aware TSK classifier matched the accuracy–fairness trade-off of a neural network with the same penalty across eight datasets.

**Keywords:** Fuzzy demographic parity; Takagi–Sugeno–Kang fuzzy systems; Algorithmic fairness; Disparity decomposition; Explainable artificial intelligence

# 1. Introduction

Machine-learning models inform credit, hiring, insurance, criminal-justice and clinical decisions, and regulators increasingly require them to be explainable and non-discriminatory [@aiact]. Fairness-aware learning formalises group criteria such as demographic parity and enforces them by pre-processing, constrained training or post-processing [@zafar; @mehrabi; @pessach; @agarwal; @zhang]. Interpretable fuzzy modelling builds rule bases whose antecedents are linguistic terms, so that a prediction can be traced to a few IF–THEN statements [@alonso; @gacto2011; @casillas; @mendel], in line with the argument that high-stakes decisions should rest on interpretable models [@rudin]. Combining the two raises two questions that neither line of work answers on its own.

**Who is in the protected group?** Group criteria assume a crisp partition, for example age ≤ 25 [@lequy]. The threshold is a convention, and the measured gap of a fixed classifier can change abruptly when it moves. Continuous attributes have been handled with dependence measures such as covariance [@zafar] or the Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi (HGR) correlation [@mary], but these penalise dependence on the attribute as a whole, not a disparity against a named group. A fuzzy protected group keeps the semantics of a group comparison and states the group as a linguistic term with an explicit membership function that a regulator can inspect and change.

**Does treating similar individuals alike remove group disparity?** A common response to proxy discrimination is to require parity *within* strata of legitimate factors, i.e. conditional statistical parity [@kamiran2013; @corbett]. In a rule-based model the rules are natural strata, and it is tempting to require that individuals who match the same rule be treated alike. It has long been known that models can reconstruct a protected attribute from correlated features [@pedreschi; @lipton] and that constraints on coarse groups can be satisfied while finer subgroups are treated unequally [@kearns]. What is missing is a statement of *when* a within-stratum constraint fails if the strata are learned by the model itself, and a measurement device that shows the failure.

We argue that a Takagi–Sugeno–Kang (TSK) rule base [@tsk] is such a device. The contributions are:

1. **Fuzzy demographic parity (FDP).** Parity with respect to a protected group given by a membership function. FDP reduces to demographic parity for crisp groups and has a covariance form (Propositions 1–2). Its plug-in estimator coincides with the probability-weighted estimator used for unobserved protected classes [@chen2019; @kallus2022]; what is new is its use as an explicit linguistic group specification, as a training objective and in the decomposition below.
2. **Rule-level decomposition.** For any convex mixture of component scores, and in particular for a TSK rule base, the parity gap splits into routing and within-rule terms (Theorem 1), with per-rule shares that do not depend on a common shift of the rule outputs.
3. **Disparity displacement.** A sufficient condition (Theorem 2, Corollary 3) shows that a rule-local penalty cannot move the group gap far from the Bayes gap when a rule base without within-rule disparity is nearly Bayes-optimal, including for regularised and approximately trained models; with a single rule, the rule-local and the group penalty coincide (Corollary 2).
4. **An empirical study** on eight public datasets, with the fuzzy group tested on three of them, and a synthetic benchmark with known Bayes scores, with dataset-level statistics, ten baselines including a fair decision tree and fair rule lists, continuous-attribute penalties, a mixture-of-experts control and a rule-level audit of Adult.

The fairness-aware TSK classifier, called FairTSK below, serves as the measurement device; it is not proposed as a more accurate classifier, and its trade-off matches that of a neural network trained with the same penalty (Section 6.1). The contribution is the definition, the decomposition and the characterisation of a failure mode of local fairness constraints, together with their empirical test; their scope and limits are discussed in Section 7.

Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 develops FDP, Section 4 the model, the decomposition and the displacement result, Section 5 the experimental protocol and Section 6 the results; Section 7 discusses limitations and Section 8 concludes. # 2. Related work

**Fairness-aware classification.** Group criteria compare decision or error rates across groups [@mehrabi; @pessach]. Pre-processing reweights or transforms the data [@kamiran]; in-processing changes the objective, through covariance constraints [@zafar], reductions to cost-sensitive learning [@agarwal], adversaries that predict the protected attribute [@zhang] or HGR penalties for continuous attributes [@mary]; post-processing adjusts group-specific thresholds [@hardt]. Interpretable-by-design methods impose parity on rule lists [@aivodji] and decision trees [@barata], and FairMOE combines interpretable and black-box experts [@fairmoe]. Fair representation learning maps inputs to an intermediate representation from which group membership is hard to recover [@zemel]; the normalised firing of a rule base is such a learned representation, and Theorem 2 concerns what happens when it is *not* constrained. None of these methods attributes a parity gap exactly to the components of the model.

**Proxies, strata and subgroups.** Redundant encodings let a model discriminate without the protected attribute [@pedreschi]. Lipton et al. [@lipton] showed that parity enforced without access to the protected attribute at prediction time is achieved through proxies of it and can induce discrimination within groups; Kearns et al. [@kearns] showed that parity on coarse groups can hide disparity on subgroups (fairness gerrymandering). Conditional statistical parity requires parity within strata of legitimate factors [@kamiran2013; @corbett]. Individual fairness [@dwork] bounds group parity by how far apart the groups' distributions are. Our displacement result is a precise instance of these concerns for a particular local constraint: parity within *learned*, soft strata, the rules of a mixture model. What the result adds is the condition under which it must occur, its exact measurement and its test where the Bayes score is known.

**Attributing disparities.** Decomposing a difference of group means into composition and treatment effects is standard in economics (Oaxaca–Blinder [@oaxaca; @blinder]) and has been revisited for causal disparity analysis [@opacic]; Theorem 1 is of this algebraic type. That an aggregate gap and the gaps within strata can differ, even in sign, is the composition effect behind Simpson's paradox, classically illustrated by graduate admissions [@bickel]. Post-hoc methods explain individual predictions in terms of features [@lime; @shap; @guidotti], and Shapley-value methods attribute a model's parity gap to input features [@begley]. These answer which *features* carry a gap; Theorem 1 answers which *components of the model* carry it and whether it arises between or within them, which is what a within-component constraint changes.

**Fuzzy rule-based classifiers and fairness.** ANFIS [@anfis] uses grid partitions whose size grows exponentially with $d$; prototype-based TSK models trained by mini-batch gradient descent scale linearly [@mbgd; @htsk; @zhou; @talpur]. Interpretability of rule bases has been studied through complexity and semantic indices [@gacto2011; @guillaume; @gacto2010; @alonso]. Fairness objectives have entered fuzzy rule-based classification only recently. Konishi et al. [@konishi] evolve fuzzy rule-based classifiers with multi-objective fuzzy genetics-based machine learning that optimises accuracy and fairness jointly, and analyse the rules of the resulting models; Krasanakis and Papadopoulos [@krasanakis] evaluate fairness *statements* in Basic fuzzy logic with crisp groups. The present paper differs from both in defining the protected group itself by a membership function and in decomposing the parity gap of a trained rule base into routing and within-rule parts. The author's earlier conference paper [@imam] argued qualitatively for combining fuzzy logic and deep learning for explainability and contains neither the fairness formulation nor the results of this paper.
